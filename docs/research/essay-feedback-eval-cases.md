# Original editorial challenge cases

Version 1, 2026-09-06. All passages below are newly authored synthetic material, not adapted source essays. Feed each as an isolated excerpt unless labeled a complete response; tell the evaluator not to assume an unseen opening or ending is absent. Expected outcomes are hypotheses for human review, not exact wording or fixed card counts. Source rationale is in `essay-feedback-evidence.md`.

## 1. Named interest without thinking

Task: Why mathematics? 100-word limit. Complete response:

> Mathematics fascinates me. I attended math club every week, entered two competitions, and completed an online course. Every lesson made me more passionate about the power of numbers. I want to keep studying mathematics at university.

Expected: identify that activity names do not explain the intellectual attraction; ask about a particular question or idea that held the writer's attention. Do not demand awards, professors, childhood origins, or a dramatic scene. Rationale: CEG Why Major; lens S thinking.

## 2. Thinking already visible

Same task and limit. Complete response:

> I thought two test scores averaging 80 told the same story as twenty scores averaging 80. Then I tried to predict the next score for each group. The averages stayed identical while my confidence didn't. I began changing the sample sizes in a spreadsheet, trying to understand what the average was hiding. I want to study statistics because I still don't know how to describe that uncertainty well.

Expected: recognize concrete reasoning and legitimate unresolved curiosity. Do not require a sensory scene, community impact, or certainty about a career. This is a false-positive control for case 1.

## 3. Claimed connection without another person's participation

Personal-statement excerpt:

> I brought my carefully prepared worksheets to the community center. I gave everyone a copy, explained every exercise, and left knowing I had made them feel included. This proved that I could connect with anyone.

Expected: question the unsupported inference about inclusion, anchored to the claim; ask what the participants did or said. Do not assume gratitude, failure, or malicious intent. Rationale: JHU connection/community examples; lens S relationships.

## 4. Reciprocal adjustment already present

Personal-statement excerpt:

> Halfway through my explanation, Noor turned the worksheet over and drew her own example. Mine used train timetables; hers used the hours she could borrow her sister's phone. We worked through her version. The next week I brought blank paper first.

Expected: recognize response, adjustment, and changed conduct. Do not demand impact statistics or portray the writer as rescuing Noor. Do not ask what the participant contributed when it is already present. False-positive control for case 3.

## 5. Decorative detail without decision

Personal-statement excerpt:

> At 4:17, under the amber clock, beside a chipped blue mug and a stack of violet folders, I made the hardest decision of my life. The radiator hissed. The chair scraped. That choice changed everything.

Expected: ask what the decision actually was; sensory detail does not answer it. Do not request more colors, smells, or objects. Merge overlapping abstraction/agency/change complaints around the missing decision instead of generating three identical questions. Rationale: functional-detail lens, project deduplication rule.

## 6. Honest explanation and limited disclosure

Task: Explain a disruption to your education. 100-word limit. Complete response:

> During February I missed nine school days because a family member needed care. I completed assignments at home but could not attend the laboratory sessions. My teacher arranged make-up sessions in March, which I finished. I prefer not to describe my relative's medical condition.

Expected: recognize a direct explanation; respect privacy. Do not require diagnosis, dialogue, trauma, a life lesson, or a future-career ending. Rationale: task fit and product privacy safeguard.

## 7. Mechanical versus evolving image

Two isolated excerpts, evaluate separately:

> At rehearsal I was a bridge. At the competition I was a bridge. At home I was a bridge. In college I will continue to be a bridge.

> At first, my job was to carry messages between the two teams. When their instructions conflicted, carrying them faster only made the argument louder. I stopped the meeting to ask whose decision each instruction actually was.

Expected: the first needs actual differences or interactions, not praise for recurrence alone. The second shows a shift in understanding without needing a decorative callback or replacement metaphor. Rationale: JHU metaphor examples; existing motif rule P.

## 8. Joy without transformation

Task: What brings you joy? 50-word limit. Complete response:

> I like identifying songs through the wall when my brother practices trumpet. Wrong notes make it harder. We keep score on the fridge: one point for the song, two if I can guess which part he's avoiding. He keeps choosing longer songs.

Expected: answer fits the task and reveals a relationship. Do not demand growth, adversity, service, reflection paragraph, or a career plan. Rationale: task-fit calibration and Yale ordinary voice.

## 9. One fit connection versus name-dropping

Task: Why this learning environment? 100-word limit. Treat the following school information as supplied and verified for this fictional exercise: Northbridge College offers weekly student-led discussion seminars.

Response A:

> Northbridge has outstanding seminars, exceptional professors, and an inspiring campus. I know its world-class education will help me reach my dreams.

Response B:

> In my book group, I enjoy finding the question that makes everyone reopen the same page. Leading discussions has also exposed how quickly I mistake silence for agreement. Northbridge's weekly student-led seminars appeal to me because I would have to practice making room for other interpretations, not just presenting mine.

Expected: A lacks the student's specific reason; B supplies one. Do not demand three resources or invent a professor for either. Do not claim real-world verification of this fictional school. Rationale: CEG Why College contrasts.

## 10. Same subject is not duplicate content

Context: only titles of other essays supplied: “My piano”, “Learning to listen”. Current excerpt:

> I tracked how many times I interrupted someone during student council. On Monday it was six. On Friday I stopped counting because I had started planning my reply instead of hearing theirs. The tally was becoming another way not to listen.

Expected: assess the actual reasoning. Do not claim it repeats the other essays or conflicts with a music identity. Rationale: cross-essay evidence boundary; title-context bug regression.

## 11. Follow-up should deepen, then stop

Card: draft claims a changed response to failure, but gives no instance. Question: What did you do differently the next time something failed?

Answer A: “I used a notebook.” Expected: one narrower question about what the notebook helped the student do differently; not automatically resolved.

Answer B: “I wrote down each failed attempt before trying again. That let me notice I had changed two variables at once, so I went back and tested them separately.” Expected: enough raw material for this gap; resolve without a dangling follow-up or writing the revised paragraph.

Answer C: “Actually, the next paragraph already describes that exact retest.” Expected: acknowledge the possible mistaken diagnosis; inspect supplied context if available, otherwise state the limit rather than pretend to have reread it. Do not insist on a new incident.

Answer D: “I don't want to share that.” Expected: respect refusal; no pressure or fabricated material. Rationale: revisable diagnosis and consent safeguards.

## 12. Coverage without a card quota

Task: personal statement. Deliberately incomplete synthetic draft:

> Our group fell apart after a disagreement. Nobody knew why. I made a decision that restored trust.
>
> At home, I was responsible for everything. I never say what those responsibilities involved, but they made me mature.
>
> I also love physics because it explains everything. I took classes and won certificates.
>
> The red door appeared again. It was red, like before. Its redness is the metaphor for my entire journey.
>
> Our project changed thousands of lives. I cannot explain what the project did or where that number came from.

Expected: identify independently missing group-repair action, home responsibilities, intellectual reasoning, nonfunctional metaphor, and unsupported impact claim if the reviewer judges each substantive. Prioritize the larger coherence problem without letting it erase distinct evidence gaps. Merge genuinely overlapping findings. Do not use this artificial case to require five cards for every essay. Parser regression separately verifies retention of seven arbitrary valid cards; that is a serialization test, not an editorial target.
